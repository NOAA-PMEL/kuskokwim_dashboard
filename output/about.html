About the Project

CONTEXT:

Temperature drives many critical processes in the Bering Sea. Seasonal sea ice, oceanographic boundaries, fish and plankton distributions, and many other marine conditions are fundamentally controlled by temperature, with major impacts on many people who rely on the Bering Sea for food, culture, or economic activity.

Yet it can be difficult for people to access real-time ocean temperature measurements, or predictions of ocean temperature.

We aim to improve accessibility of ocean temperature information in the Bering Sea region.

MOTIVATION:

As a starting point, our team of scientists and fishery coordinators joined together to closely examine ocean temperatures in the inner shelf area of Kuskokwim Bay. This area supports important local small-scale fisheries, including subsistence harvest of halibut, and also supports a large portion of the commercial yellowfin sole fishery. The yellowfin sole fishery can include halibut bycatch, which is a problem for the closely-managed commercial fishery and detrimental for local residents.

The most efficient way to reduce bycatch is to intentionally fish for target species when non-target species are not present. In the case of yellowfin sole, this means fishing when bottom temperatures are still cold enough to act as a barrier for halibut— about 2°C or less. But how can fishery captains predict what bottom temperatures will be in a region hundreds of miles from their port in Dutch Harbor? We addressed that challenge as a team, and these ‘dashboard’ webpages are the result.

METHODS:

    We deployed a range of oceanographic instruments in the Kuskokwim Bay region:

  • Pop-up buoys that collect bottom temperature data for months at a time, then ‘pop up’ to the surface where they transmit the stored data back to NOAA and UW offices in Seattle
  • Moorings that are fixed in place for about four months during the summer period, collecting a range of data (including temperature) from the bottom to the surface
  • Scientific-grade temperature sensors attached to commercial fishing nets, collecting data across the water column as the nets descend and ascend, and near-bottom temperatures as the net moves horizontally while actively fishing
